Last week, I began solving this problem set: [CSES](cses.fi/problemset/list/). Nowadays, I'm going through this phase of total disillusionment with the current curriculum and pedagogical techniques in Indian colleges. I have realized how valuable and, in fact, thrilling solving programming problems is. But we students have all been robbed of this by trading our skills for an assured future with a nice pay. I am unsure if this is really 'assured' - doubtful, but I am sure that such stakes push students to forget about feeling the thrill of solving a problem and continually focus on that big mountain they have to conquer to sit for interviews (and actually crack them). I hate this whole enterprise, often dubbed the 'rat race'.  
Nonetheless, I started DS&A afresh last week and chose this CSES set to practice with. In this essay, I really want to discuss my observations and conclusions from this past week.  
These are arranged as a series of question and their answers.

***

**Q:** What do I notice first?   
I think the first thing I notice is the nature of the expected output. This is because that would help me judge how much effort solving a problem would take. Moreover, it gives a subtle idea of what existing techniques and algorithms might fit the question. But I really think noticing just the nature of the output doesn't reveal much. I need to take the scenario, constraints, input, etc into account as well. Only then can a fairly accurate judgement be made. 
<br/>
**Q:** What makes a problem suddenly become tractable?  
Patterns. Solving programming problems feels partly like an empiricist endeavor. Like we start with two things - the problem description and some sample examples. From these two data points, we derive a solution. 
[For some reason, this really feels akin to investigations as they show on TV. For real, while writing this essay, I am feeling like Carl Jung writing about dream analysis. Haha.]
But patterns have a catch. Sometimes, we may get really thrilled that we cracked the matrix; we have a valid pattern. But then we realize we are in Plato's cave. The patterns found may be valid only for certain scenarios and not 'generally'. But again, this is fine - this pattern itself would serve as a data point alongside our other findings to determine a more general pattern.  
<br/>
**Q** Is brute force useful as a starting point? 
Brute force is the worst viable solution to the problem. But it is valuable since we know how the problem can be solved, even if the solution is not feasible for bigger scales. One - we have solved the problem; two - we have found another data point to lead us to a better and more optimized solution. But I agree sometimes, we may feel it rather like a detour rather than a stepping stone. I will talk more about this in the next answers.
<br/>
**Q** How do I know what information matters and what doesn't?  
I don't think I am that mature as a problem solver right now. I mean, I can pretend I am listing a few things that would make sense, but I know I am not. I have often talked about this with my friends as well. If I really had this mature eye, I could produce the judgment in the first answer right away. I cannot. I struggle to judge whether a simple mathematical formula would suffice or the problem requires a whole backtracking kinda enumeration of all possible scenarios.  
Therefore, what I do is this: I religiously follow the leads that each piece of information seems to give me, even if eventually I am bound to hit a wall. I assume every hint is 'the one'. This gives me the motivation to do everything I can do. Gradually, I believe I will know which road to take and which not.  
<br/>
**Q** What does “getting stuck” actually look like?
IMO, 'getting stuck' is really, most of the time, just mental fog. Nowadays, given all the distractions and young-adult things happening in life, they really make our brains hazy. And sometimes, it is just bewilderment. I mean, we rarely give our fullest while solving a problem. Sometimes, we just want pieces to fall into place on their own. We really need to come out of our zones and act. 
To resolve this, what I do is - I pour my mind onto paper. I write what exactly is stopping me from getting to the answer. It brings my consciousness back to place. It makes my mind more aware of itself.
 Other times, however - as I said in the brute force answer -  we hit a genuine wall. Like, while doing one question, it was throwing a TLE (Time Limit Exceeded), indicating I can really reduce the time it is taking to run. But I didn't have the slightest idea where the hell these unwanted efforts were going. Then I gave in. I asked ChatGPT - it told me it can actually be solved pretty easily and with lower time complexity with one graph algorithm. I did everything right - figuring out the pattern, dry running, coming up with a backtracking approach but it was just not the best way to see the problem. That is exactly why we need theory. We have to know what clever techniques computer scientists have invented to bypass these roadblocks.   
<br/>

***

Well, this wraps up the whole intent of today's essay. These are my observations and conclusions. I really hope I myself am on the correct path and not where I would hit a roadblock. Anyways, it would still be a learning experience and a data point to come up with a better methodology. Haha.
